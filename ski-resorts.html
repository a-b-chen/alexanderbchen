The resorts

Twelve mountains, and what each one taught me

The East

Where it all started — Vermont and the hills around Pittsburgh.

Mount Snow Vermont · the first one

The very first one. I was eight, in a rental jacket, spending more time sitting in the snow than skiing on it. Mount Snow is where the whole thing started — every skier has a bunny hill they remember, and this was mine.

Smugglers’ Notch Vermont · where parallel clicked

Where parallel finally clicked. Somewhere on those trails I stopped skiing in a wedge and started actually turning — edge to edge, the way it’s meant to feel. The jump from pizza to parallel is the first real graduation in skiing, and Smuggs is where I earned it.

Seven Springs Pennsylvania · the home hill

The biggest of the local hills and the one I skied most. Not a huge mountain, but enough to get laps in after school and on weekends. A lot of my early reps happened right here.

Hidden Valley Pennsylvania · local

Small, close, and reliable — the kind of place you go when you just want to ski for a couple of hours. Plenty of ordinary afternoons here that added up to more than they seemed to at the time.

Boyce Park Pennsylvania · five minutes away

About as local as it gets: a little hill with a few runs and some lights. Nobody’s bucket list has Boyce Park on it, but it’s close and it’s open at night, and that counts for a lot when you’re just trying to get better.

The West

Every winter break, and the trips that made me actually good.

Jackson Hole Wyoming · the cast trip

The trip I did with a cast on my left elbow. Jackson is steep, serious, no-nonsense terrain, and skiing it one-armed was almost certainly not what the doctor ordered. But watching from the lodge was never really on the table. I took it slow and careful and came home in one piece — well, one and a half.

Vail Colorado · every winter break

Colorado is my winter-break ritual, and Vail is the heart of it. Enormous, endless, and exactly the kind of mountain you need to actually improve: back bowls, trees, and groomers that run forever. Four years of Decembers here did most of the work of turning me into a real skier.

Breckenridge Colorado · the high one

Part of the same Colorado rotation. High, cold, and steep up top — the kind of altitude that reminds you to breathe. Some of my favorite above-treeline runs are here.

Keystone Colorado · night laps

The third of the Colorado regulars, and the one I’ll ski well past dark. Long rolling runs and night skiing under the lights — a good place to just put in mileage and enjoy it.

Alta Utah · powder, skiers only

Utah powder, and a skiers-only mountain that leans all the way into the mythology. Alta gets the light, dry snow everyone talks about, and on the right morning it’s about as good as skiing gets. No snowboarders, no apologies.

Snowbird Utah · the deep days

Right next door to Alta and every bit as deep. Steeper and more aggressive, with tram-served terrain that doesn’t let up. The powder days here are the ones I quietly measure everything else against.

British Columbia

Whistler Blackcomb British Columbia · every spring break

Every spring break for four years, and the mountain that’s pushed me hardest. Whistler is where carving finally made sense — tipping the skis over until they hook up and pull you through the arc. It’s also where I got greedy: I took The Saddle at something like eighty miles an hour, lost control, and crashed as hard as I ever have. Worth every second. I’d do it again — maybe a little slower.
