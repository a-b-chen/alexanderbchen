Mathematics

Outworking the talent everyone said I didn’t have

Stubborn, not gifted

I’ll be honest about something up front: I was never the naturally gifted one. The kids who saw something, who finished the test with twenty minutes to spare and looked bored doing it -- that was never me. I was slow, dull-witted, maybe even a little dumb. I couldn't recognize patterns, made a bunch of silly mistakes, and had a slow reaction. But eventually, I realized that what I did have instead was a stubborn refusal to believe that “not yet” meant “not ever.”

Early on, a teacher pulled me aside and told me, plainly, to stop trying. I’d never make the AIME. I didn’t have the talent for it, and I was wasting time I could spend on something I was actually good at. He wasn’t being cruel about it -- I think he believed he was saving me from disappointment. And the hard part is that he was half right. I didn’t have the talent. I just decided that wasn’t the whole sentence, that it wasn't something that another person could decide for me.

It wasn’t only teachers. Some of the hardest arguments happened at home. My parents were very supportive at first, but they also eventually grew tired. For a period of time, I was convinced that I wasn't built for math. There were a lot of long, frustrating conversations -- more than a few I’m not proud of -- where my parents told me to try something else, that there was something better for me to do but I kept defending a thing I couldn’t yet prove was worth anything. When you have nothing to show for it, “trust me” is a terrible argument, and I made it constantly.

So I worked. Not in the inspiring-montage way -- in the boring, repetitive, one-more-problem-set way. I started competition math in sixth grade with Dr. Shi, and for years getting better just meant more repitition: more problems, more dead ends, more evenings stuck on something everyone else seemed to find obvious. I wasn’t fast. I was just very hard to discourage for long.

The results came eventually, and slowly. I made the AIME -- the exact thing I’d been told was out of reach -- and eventually scored a 12 on it, the highest in my school’s history. I qualified for the USAMO, the first student from my school ever to do it. Gold on the USAMTS. A national title with MathLeague. None of it came from some talent I finally unlocked. It came from the hours, and nothing else. And when the results finally showed up, they made a case at home that I never could have won with words.

Somewhere in there, the medals stopped being the point. Mrs. Boyer, the school Math Team advisor once dared me to actually learn multivariable calculus, and I came back with a 360-page textbook on it that my school now teaches from. I co-founded SSAMO and built our math club into something that competes nationally, mostly because I wanted the younger kids to have the head start I had to fight for. I wanted to hand them an opportunity that I was never given.

Last summer I spent weeks at the Ross program working through number theory with no answer key at all -- just the problem, and however long it took to actually understand it.

I still don’t walk into a room as the most talented person in it, and I’ve made my peace with that. What I’ve figured out is that talent sets where you start, not where you finish, and that being willing to outwork the doubt -- your teachers’, your parents’, and on the bad days your own -- carries you a lot further than people want to admit.

I don’t hold that teacher’s comment against him anymore. In a strange way I owe him for it: he handed me a very specific thing to prove wrong, and setting out to prove it wrong is how I learned the way I actually work. It turns out I never needed the talent. I just needed to not stop.

Even as my career in high-school level olympiads has drawn to an end, there's always more things to do. I'm very interested in research, though I'm not sure in what specific direction. All this time I've been bring math along with me; it's about time to see where math will take me.
