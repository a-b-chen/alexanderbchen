Swimming

The solitude of the grind, the rush of the race

Quiet mornings, loud afternoons

I’ve been in the water since I was five, right around when I first sat down at a piano, so the two have been running side by side my whole life. It’s the one habit I’ve never really broken -- there’s almost always been a black line to follow before even school.

Most of my swimming is club swimming: year-round USA Swimming, early practices, and long sets that only my coach is watching. It’s mostly you against the clock or against other friends, and the clock and even your friends don’t care how you feel that morning, especially when they're racing you. Man, if you're not feeling well, your friends will take every chance to destroy you that one practice. That’s where the base gets built. Twice it took me to the Eastern Zone Long Course Age Group Championships, which was the first time the miles really felt like they added up to something.

High school swimming is completely different. Suddenly it’s a team, it’s scored, and there’s a whole deck of people yelling for a race that’s over in under a minute. Swimming for Shady Side, we took back-to-back section titles for the first time in school history, and I’ve made it to the WPIAL championships three years running and the PIAA state meet twice.

The relays are the part I’d never trade. Four people, one combined time, and a very direct kind of accountability -- nobody wants to be the leg that let the other three down. It’s the closest swimming gets to a real team sport, and after a season of solitary mornings, it feels like the reward.

Club and varsity pull in opposite directions -- one quiet and internal, the other loud and collective -- and I’ve needed both. The grind is what makes the racing possible, and the racing is what makes the grind worth it. A hard morning set also has a way of making the rest of the day feel easy.

An unfortunate part of my swimming career is the retirement of my old head coach. I switched teams, but it never quite felt the same. It took a significant amount of time to adjust, especially moving from a small, tight family to a rather large community. I was in practices with even faster people, and swam even more yardage a day.

People ask if it ever gets boring, staring at the bottom of a pool for two hours. It doesn’t, really -- same as with piano, every day in the water is a slightly different version of me showing up, and I’ve come to like who that turns me into.

The difficulty with swim is the consistency. Not showing up for even a couple of days ruins the self-motivation, discipline, and the years of momentum built up. It's hard to go back, from vacation to diving into a freezing pool working yourself to the physical limit for hours on end every day. But at the end of the day, the sport is part of who I am, and I can't quite say no to practice when I have a caring coach threatening to make me swim the same number of 200 fly's for every practice I've missed.
